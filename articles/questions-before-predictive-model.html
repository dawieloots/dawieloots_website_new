Five questions to ask before commissioning a predictive model

Before worrying about algorithms, make sure the business problem is worth modelling.

1. What decision will the model improve?

If the model predicts something accurately but nobody will change what they do because of the prediction, the model may have little commercial value. Start with the decision.

2. What does a mistake cost?

Models are rarely perfect. A false positive and a false negative may have completely different consequences. Understanding the cost of errors helps determine what “good enough” means.

3. Do you have the right data?

Historical data is not automatically useful modelling data. You need a meaningful outcome, relevant observations and information that would genuinely have been available at prediction time. Data leakage can make a model look brilliant while being useless.

4. Is there enough signal?

Sometimes the answer is simply no. If an outcome is largely random, driven by unobserved factors, or based on too few historical cases, a sophisticated algorithm will not manufacture predictive power.

5. What happens after the model is built?

A model is not a finished product. Someone needs to receive the prediction, act on it and monitor performance. The best predictive model improves a real decision repeatedly.
